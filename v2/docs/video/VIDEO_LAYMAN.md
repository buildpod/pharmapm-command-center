## Scene 1 — A story everyone recognises (0:00–0:40)

Think of any big project you've watched from the outside — a new IT system, an
office move, a product launch. For months, the updates are green. Everything is
"on track". Then, close to the deadline, the truth arrives all at once: it's
late, it costs more, and somehow nobody saw it coming.

Here's the thing — usually nobody lied. The people running it were doing their
best. The problem is they were reporting with tools that can't tell the
difference between a project that's fine and a project that only *looks* fine.

## Scene 2 — Why the usual tools can't help (0:40–1:15)

Most projects are tracked in a spreadsheet or a planning tool. And in those, a
person types in the status. They type the dates. They type "eighty percent
done."

Now, imagine a bank statement where you could type your own balance. It would
tell you what you hoped, not what you had. That's a project plan: a record of
intentions, typed by people who very much want it to go well.

Nobody in that chain is being dishonest. But the report can never be proof of
anything, because it was written by hand.

## Scene 3 — The idea (1:15–1:55)

So we built something different. It's called PharmaPM, and it's for the people
running big, heavily-regulated projects — like a pharmaceutical company putting
in a new system that regulators will inspect.

The idea is simple: instead of asking people to write a status report, the
system works it out. It looks at the actual records — the work, the deadlines,
the money spent, the approvals signed — and it calculates the health of the
project itself.

And critically: **there's no box where you can type a better number.** The only
way to make the score go up is to make the project actually go better.

## Scene 4 — The clever part (1:55–2:35)

Here's my favourite bit. Everyone knows the classic project fib — "we're
ninety percent done" — when really ninety percent of the *easy* work is done.

So the system checks the claim against things you can't easily fudge: how much
money has actually been spent, which milestones have genuinely been signed off.
If someone says the work is nearly finished but almost nothing has been spent
and no milestone has been passed, it says so — right there in the report.

It doesn't accuse anyone. It just quietly notes that the numbers don't line up
yet and suggests checking. A report that admits where it's shaky turns out to
be far more trustworthy than one that's all green.

## Scene 5 — What you end up with (2:35–3:00)

What you get is a project update where every single claim can be traced back to
the evidence behind it. If someone asks "how do you know?", the answer is one
click, not a week of digging.

It's a simple idea, really: a dashboard that can't flatter you. For projects
where being wrong is genuinely expensive, that turns out to be worth a lot.
