# Company / buyer video — the business case

## Scene 1 — The problem, in your numbers (0:00–0:50)

A validated system implementation — Veeva, SAP, a new quality platform — runs
for twelve to twenty-four months and costs millions. When one of these slips,
it rarely slips visibly. It drifts: a configuration sprint runs two weeks over,
a migration dry-run gets deferred, validation compresses to absorb it. Status
stays green because every individual step still looks recoverable.

Then the go-live date moves, and the conversation starts with "when did we know
this?" — and nobody can answer, because the evidence is scattered across
inboxes, spreadsheets, and three versions of the plan.

The cost isn't only the delay. It's the credibility of every future status
report your programme produces.

## Scene 2 — Why your current tooling can't fix it (0:50–1:40)

This isn't a discipline problem. It's structural.

Your Gantt tool and your spreadsheets record *intent* — every date and every
percent-complete is typed by someone accountable for how it reads. Your ticket
tracker manages *throughput* — it tells you what the team is doing, not whether
the promise holds. Neither has a frozen commitment, an independent check on
reported progress, or an evidence trail an auditor accepts.

So the honest summary is: the tools can describe your plan, but they cannot
defend it. And in a regulated programme, defending it is the job.

## Scene 3 — What we do differently (1:40–3:10)

Four things, and each one closes a specific gap.

**One — the verdict is computed.** Delivery confidence comes from earned value:
planned work, earned work, actual spend. There is no field to type a healthier
number, and progress is weighted by budget, so finishing ten trivial tasks
cannot look like delivery. If the data is too thin to judge, it says so rather
than inventing a verdict.

**Two — the commitment is frozen.** The go-live date the programme committed to
is captured. If it moves, the system records who moved it, when, and why — and
that appears in the next brief. The target can move; it cannot wander.

**Three — consequences are shown before you commit.** Change a date and the
impact review opens first: downstream work, milestone movement, whether the
commitment is breached, whether it collides with a regulatory freeze. Accepting
a slip becomes a named, logged decision that lands in the report — not a quiet
edit.

**Four — the claim is cross-checked.** Reported progress is tested against
independent signals: implied cost efficiency, gates actually reached, spend
actually recorded. Where they disagree, the report says so in writing. A pack
that flags its own weak spots is the one your steering committee can rely on.

## Scene 4 — Governance and data posture (3:10–4:00)

Every claim on every screen opens the records behind it — tasks, risks,
approvals, cost lines — and the reports read those same records, so the pack and
the working plan cannot diverge. The audit trail, approval chain, decision log,
and readiness gates are the architecture, not features bolted on afterwards.

On data: project records stay in your environment. There is no vendor cloud
processing your delivery data. For a quality organisation assessing a supplier,
that removes an entire category of question.

## Scene 5 — How to start (4:00–4:40)

You don't start with a rollout, a licence negotiation, or a change programme.
You start with one project and one reporting cycle.

We take a real plan — ideally a troubled one — import it, run a full cycle
through the engine, and produce the report. You compare that against the status
pack you received last month. The difference is the entire evaluation.

If you have a programme where the dashboard is green but your instinct isn't:
that is the one to run it on.
