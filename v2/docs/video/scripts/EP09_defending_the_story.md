# Episode 9 — Defending the story

## The principle

Traceability is what converts a claim into evidence.

Two requirements. Any number must lead to the records that produced it. And
history must show who changed what, when, and why.

In regulated environments this is formalised — audit trail requirements under
Part 11, and the data-integrity principles that say a record should be
attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate. But the
underlying idea is older and simpler: if you cannot show your working, you have
an opinion, not a finding.

## Why it goes wrong

Someone in a review asks: "where does that number come from?"

And the honest answer is that it came from a spreadsheet that came from a
conversation that came from someone's estimate three weeks ago. Answering
properly means a week of email archaeology, and by then the review has moved on.

So the number stands unchallenged — or it gets quietly distrusted, which is
worse, because now nothing in the pack carries weight.

## How PharmaPM implements it

Three mechanisms, and you've seen all of them by now.

Every number is a door. KPI cards, the signals on Delivery Signals, the
governance controls — each links to the source records. Not a summary of them;
the actual tasks, risks, documents, and cost lines.

The activity feed is the audit log made readable. Every mutation is captured;
the feed presents it chronologically as human sentences, so "what changed since
we last spoke?" is a page, not an investigation.

And re-baselines are visible. If the committed go-live moves, the system records
who moved it, when, and the reason given — and surfaces it in the report's
baseline changes section.

## Doing it

**Delivery Signals.** Open any active signal. Each one carries why it matters,
the next action, and trace chips. Click a trace chip and you land on the exact
record that produced the signal. Practise this once — it's the move you'll make
live in a review.

**Command, then Activity.** Read what has changed since the last project
conversation. This is a good five-minute habit before any status meeting.

**Plan, then Milestones.** If the commitment genuinely needs to move, use the
commitment banner to re-baseline — and give a real reason. The banner shows
drift between what was committed and where the forecast now sits, alongside the
history of previous re-baselines.

## What good looks like

In a review, a challenge gets answered in one click rather than becoming an
action item.

That changes the character of the meeting. When evidence is instantly available,
the conversation moves off "is this number right?" and onto "what are we going
to do about it?" — which is the conversation you actually needed.

## Common mistakes

The serious one: re-baselining to make drift disappear.

You can move the target. The system will let you, because sometimes a
re-baseline is the honest thing to do — scope changed, a dependency failed, the
original date was wrong. But it is recorded, it carries your name and your
stated reason, and it appears in the report.

A project with four undisclosed re-baselines was never late. A project with four
*recorded* ones is telling a story about itself, and everyone can read it. Only
one of those is governance.

## Closing the series

Across these episodes: you set up a project with an agreed commitment, made the
plan real with owners and gates, brought money in so progress reflects value,
learned why claimed progress gets cross-checked, absorbed change as governed
decisions, kept registers an auditor accepts, proved readiness with evidence,
reported from records rather than memory, and defended every claim with a trace.

None of that is exotic project management. It is ordinary discipline that most
tools make optional — and this one makes structural.

That is the whole idea.
