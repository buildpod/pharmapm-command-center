# Set A — What PharmaPM is (concept deck)

The **single source of truth for explaining the product**. Eleven slides.
Every outward-facing asset derives from here — the layman video, the company
video, the pitch, the website copy. Change the story here, regenerate the rest.

Set B (`TRAINING_CURRICULUM.md`) is the separate "how to use it" track. This
deck never teaches operation; it explains what the thing is and why it exists.

**Per slide:** headline · the point in one breath · what's on screen · the
proof (so no claim floats free).

---

## A1 — Projects don't fail loudly. They fail quietly.

**The point.** Regulated implementations rarely blow up. They drift: the plan
slips a little, the status stays green, and the slip surfaces months later when
it is most expensive to fix.

**On screen.** A green status pack next to a go-live date that has quietly
moved three times.

**Proof.** Every delivery leader has lived this. The pattern is universal:
nobody lied, but nobody could see it either.

---

## A2 — The tools record intent. They can't defend it.

**The point.** Spreadsheets, Gantt charts, and ticket trackers describe a plan.
But every date, every percent-complete, every RAG colour is typed by a human
with a stake in how it reads — so the plan cannot be evidence for itself.

**On screen.** A cell being edited; the RAG dot flipping from amber to green by
hand.

**Proof.** There is no field in Excel or MS Project that a project manager
cannot change to make the picture look better. That is not a criticism of the
people; it is a property of the tool.

---

## A3 — So we built a truth layer.

**The point.** One set of records drives the plan, the dashboard, and the
report. Numbers are computed from the records — not typed. Every change is
logged. The output is a delivery story that survives being questioned.

**On screen.** The layer diagram: records → computation → dashboard + report.

**Proof.** This is the whole thesis; the next five slides are how it is
enforced.

---

## A4 — The verdict is computed, not claimed.

**The point.** The headline confidence score comes from earned value — what you
planned to have done, what you actually earned, and what you spent. There is no
field where anyone can type a healthier number.

**On screen.** Executive Verdict card: "Watch · 73/100 · schedule is the main
drag."

**Proof.** If the data is too thin to judge, it says *pending* instead of
inventing a verdict. A brand-new plan reads **"Plan only"** — because a plan
that hasn't started hasn't earned trust, it just hasn't deviated yet.

---

## A5 — The promise is frozen.

**The point.** The go-live date you commit to is captured and measured against.
If it moves, the system records who moved it, when, and why — and puts that in
the next report.

**On screen.** The commitment banner showing committed date vs live forecast,
with re-baseline history.

**Proof.** This closes the most common way trouble hides: quietly moving the
target so the project is never late.

---

## A6 — Consequences are shown before you commit.

**The point.** Change a date and the impact review opens *first*: what shifts
downstream, which gates move, whether the commitment is breached, whether it
collides with a freeze window. You accept or adjust — explicitly.

**On screen.** The impact drawer mid-decision.

**Proof.** Accepting is recorded as a decision with your name on it and appears
in the report's "Accepted changes". Slips still happen — they just can't happen
silently.

---

## A7 — The claim itself gets checked.

**The point.** Self-reported progress is the most optimistic number in project
management. The system cross-checks it against independent signals — implied
cost efficiency, gates actually reached, money actually spent — and flags the
gap.

**On screen.** The integrity caveat on a report that otherwise looks fine.

**Proof.** It flags, never silently discounts. A pack that surfaces its own weak
spots is more believable than one that is uniformly green — and sponsors know
it.

---

## A8 — Every number is a door.

**The point.** Any figure on any screen opens the records that produced it —
the tasks, risks, documents, cost lines. Reports read the same records, so the
pack and the working plan cannot tell different stories.

**On screen.** A KPI click-through into its source records.

**Proof.** "Says who?" is answered in one click instead of a week of email
archaeology.

---

## A9 — What you actually get: a report you can defend.

**The point.** The deliverable is not a tool licence — it is a status pack where
every line traces to evidence: the verdict and why, accepted changes, baseline
movements, the integrity caveat, and direction of travel since last week.

**On screen.** The SteerCo brief, printed.

**Proof.** Three audiences, one record set: Weekly Status, Steering Committee,
Workstream detail. Print to PDF, export to Excel.

---

## A10 — Built for regulated delivery.

**The point.** Audit trail, approvals, decision log, and readiness gates are the
architecture — not add-ons. And the project data stays on your machine: no
vendor cloud, no third-party processing.

**On screen.** The audit feed, and the readiness gates computed from approvals.

**Proof.** For pharma delivery data, "nothing leaves your environment" is a
compliance feature your quality team will recognise, not a limitation.

---

## A11 — How you start.

**The point.** You don't start with a software rollout. You start with one
project: import the real plan, run one reporting cycle, and compare the report
you get against the one you got last month.

**On screen.** The ask, plainly.

**Proof.** If you have a project where the dashboard is green but your instinct
isn't — that's the one to run it on.
